2 · Overview

The company and the market

What it is

Quillmesh is a Lisbon-based developer-tools company founded in 2022. It makes qmc, an open-source contract-testing CLI for event-driven systems (Kafka, NATS and AMQP), and Quillmesh Cloud, a paid hosted service built on top of it. The pitch in one line: catch breaking changes between services that talk through events before they reach production, not after.

Its buyers are platform engineering teams at companies running dozens of services. Its users are the backend engineers who write the producers and consumers.

How it works

Each service declares what it produces and what it consumes in a contract file, quill.yaml, with message schemas in JSON Schema or Avro. A producer contract says: I publish order.created with these fields. A consumer contract says: I read order.created and I rely on id, total and currency.

In CI, qmc verify checks a change against the contracts on the other side. Removing a field a consumer relies on, changing a type, or narrowing an enum is flagged as breaking. qmc can-i-deploy then answers the release question: is this version compatible with what runs in each environment? Version 3.0 adds replay mode, which records a sample of real production events and replays them against a new consumer before it ships (docs).

The idea to say out loud: a schema registry checks that a producer's change is compatible with older versions of itself. Contract testing checks it against what each consumer actually uses. That consumer view is the moat.

Business model

Open core. The qmc CLI is free and Apache-2.0 licensed. Revenue comes from Quillmesh Cloud: a hosted contract registry, a compatibility matrix across all services, deploy history and alerts. The Team plan costs $29 per connected service per month and is free up to 5 services; Enterprise adds single sign-on, audit logs and an on-premises registry at custom prices.

The funnel is bottom-up: an engineer adds qmc to one pipeline, the team hits the coordination problem around 5 services, and Cloud becomes the shared source of truth. That is why developer relations sits close to revenue here: CLI activation is the top of the funnel.

3 · Quick Q&A

Quick questions and model answers

Say your answer out loud first, then open the model answer.

Ecosystem and product

1What problem does contract testing solve in event-driven systems?

In request and response APIs a breaking change fails loudly at call time. With events the producer does not know who listens, so a removed field breaks a consumer silently, sometimes hours later. Contract testing makes the hidden dependency explicit and checks it in CI, before deploy.

2Schema registry versus contract testing, in two sentences?

A registry enforces compatibility rules on a producer's schema against its own earlier versions. Contract testing checks a change against what each consumer actually reads, so it allows a change nobody depends on and blocks one that looks harmless but breaks a real reader.

3What does can-i-deploy answer?

Whether this exact version of a service is compatible with the versions running in a given environment, using the verification results stored in the registry. It turns contract testing from a report into a release gate.

4What is replay mode and why does it matter?

Shipped in v3.0 in September 2026, it records a sample of real production events and replays them against a new consumer version in CI. Contracts catch what teams declared; replay catches what they forgot to declare, like a field that is optional in the schema but always present in practice.

The role's craft: developer relations

5How do you measure developer relations in an open-core company?

Tie activity to the funnel: CLI installs and first successful qmc verify (activation), teams with 5 or more services verified (the Cloud trigger), community-sourced Cloud signups, and contributor retention. Report a small set every month and say which ones you influence directly. Talk attendance goes in an appendix.

6What makes a good quickstart for a CLI like qmc?

Time to first value under 10 minutes, a working example the user did not have to write, and an error message on the first breaking change that teaches the concept. Your Zelvora quickstart rewrite is the proof you know how: 3 days to 40 minutes.

7How do you turn community feedback into product input without becoming a ticket router?

Aggregate and rank: a monthly friction report with counts, quotes and a proposed fix, reviewed live with product. You did exactly this at Zelvora, and 7 of the items shipped in 2025.

8How do you choose which conferences to attend?

Go where buyers and builders overlap: platform engineering and streaming events rather than general developer festivals. Judge each event by the activations that follow, using a workshop repo or a tracked link, not by badge scans.

The company

9Why per-service pricing instead of per seat?

Value grows with the number of services under contract, not with the number of people who log in. Per-service pricing also removes the incentive to share one login, and it maps to the moment Cloud becomes necessary, around 5 services.

10What happened with the v2.7.0 release?

For two days in June 2026, enum narrowing was not flagged as breaking. They shipped v2.7.1, published a postmortem with a timeline and added the case to their test corpus. For a trust product, the open postmortem was the right response, and it is a good talking point.

11What was the August community controversy about?

A proposal to show the cross-service compatibility matrix only in Cloud. Contributors read it as moving value out of the open-source tool. The CTO committed to keeping every check in the CLI open and moving only the hosted view. It is the open-core boundary question in one thread.

12Who is the buyer and who is the user?

The buyer is the platform engineering lead who owns release safety across teams. The user is the backend engineer writing producers and consumers. Developer relations wins the user; the user's success builds the buyer's case.

Likely objections and honest reframes

“This is a lead role and you have never managed anyone.”

Reframe: True, no direct reports. What you have led: a volunteer organizing team of 4 for five years, where nobody had to follow you, and a monthly cross-functional review with product. Ask how the role grows; if the plan is to hire two people in year one, say you want a hiring plan and a manager to learn from in the first quarter.

“Payments is not developer tools.”

Reframe: Your users were developers integrating an events API, and their hardest problems were the ones Quillmesh solves: unclear event contracts and breaking changes. Before that you ran a Kafka pipeline and built the CI compatibility check that qmc replaces.

“Have you worked in open source with a commercial edge?”

Reframe: Not in an open-core company, and say so. You maintain a small library and you have read the August thread closely. Your view: keep every check open, sell the coordination layer. Ask how they draw that line internally.
